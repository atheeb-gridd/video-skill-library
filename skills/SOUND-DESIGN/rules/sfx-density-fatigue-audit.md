# SFX overload — the density audit that keeps the viewer's brain out of fatigue

## What it is
The first named sound-design mistake in the source corpus, and the one with a stated deadline: a mix carrying an accent *"every other second, in every other frame"* tires the viewer's brain **within two or three minutes**, after which *"either the viewer pays attention, or they just get distracted by the sound effects."* That last clause is the important one — this is framed as a **retention** problem, not a taste problem. Attention becomes zero-sum: the sound design starts competing with the content it was added to support, and it wins for a while and then loses everything.

Two research lines explain the two halves of the failure:

- **Habituation explains why constant accents stop working.** *"An increase in the frequency of stimulus presentation (i.e., shorter interstimulus interval) will increase the rate of habituation"* — the faster you fire accents, the faster they stop producing a response. Worse, habituation generalises: *"habituation to an original stimulus will also occur to other stimuli that are similar to the original stimulus (stimulus generalization)"*, so swapping whoosh A for the very similar whoosh B does not restore the response. And *"organisms may habituate to repeated sudden loud noises when they learn that these have no consequences"* — which is the mechanism behind the corpus's other rule, that risers used when nothing important follows *"lose their credibility and stop working."*
- **Cognitive load explains the fatigue itself.** Working memory is *"extremely limited in both capacity and duration"*, and extraneous load is the load *"generated by the manner in which information is presented."* Every accent the viewer has to parse is extraneous load spent on presentation instead of on the content. Two or three minutes is a plausible timescale for that debt to become subjectively tiring.

This note is the **measurement**, and that is what separates it from its two neighbours. [[struct-stimulation-budget]] sets the SFX-per-minute census the format is allowed *before* anything is placed. [[sfx-placement-discipline]] is the per-event admission gate that decides whether one candidate earns its slot. **This note runs last, on the finished mix, and answers a different question: given everything that actually got placed, is the result over the line?** It exists because per-event decisions are individually defensible and collectively fatal — every whoosh had a reason, and there are still forty of them in ninety seconds.

The audit has independent axes, and a video can fail on any one of them:

1. **Rate** — accents per minute. The headline number.
2. **Inter-onset interval** — how *close together* the accents are, and how evenly. The real driver, and the one the source's phrasing describes: "every other second" is an IOI of about 2 s, and he calls that already too much.
3. **Repetition** — how often the *same* file recurs, and how similar the substitutes are.
4. **Salience** — how loud the accents are relative to dialogue; the same rate at aesthetic level (−20 dB) is far less fatiguing than at motion level (−12 dB).
5. **Spectral clustering** — how much of the accent energy sits in the same band. Ten effects spread across the spectrum tire less than five that all live in the 2–8 kHz "tick" region.
6. **Loudness range and crest factor** — whether the accents have been flattened by limiting, which is the other half of listener fatigue: compression artefacts *"become more apparent"* as level rises and push the listener to disengage.

**Read the density bands together with the counting rule that produced them — this is the one place the two halves of this note genuinely disagreed, and the disagreement is an artefact of method, not of judgement.** A four-layer impact is *four* onsets to a transient detector and *one* accent to a clip ledger, so the two procedures below yield numbers that differ by roughly 2–3×. Both band tables are given, each labelled with the count it applies to. Never compare a number from one against the thresholds of the other.

On timescale, be honest about the evidence. Listener fatigue is a real working term but *"not a clinically recognized state"*; the causes cited include sensory overload from *"a multitude of sounds from several different sources"* and dynamic-range-compression artefacts, and one cited study had subjects report fatigue after **six minutes of a metronome** — a perfectly regular accent stream, in a lab, with nothing else to attend to. The source's two-to-three-minute figure for video, where the accents sit on top of speech and picture, is plausible and stricter. Treat **2–6 minutes** as the onset window, and take the operational consequence: fatigue is **cumulative**, so the audit runs on every minute of the runtime, not on one sample.

**Style.** No `sfx/` style tag: the density budget is counted across all three styles at once, because a diegetic click, a motion whoosh and an aesthetic riser all spend from the same attention budget. Aesthetic accents are simply the group that overspends first ([[sfx-intensify-without-referent]]).

## When to use it
- **As the last gate of the sound pass, on the rendered mix, before delivery. Every project.** It is cheap and it is the only pass that can *remove* work.
- Whenever the video is longer than about two minutes — under that, overload has less time to accumulate and the per-event gate is usually sufficient.
- **Immediately after any pass that added sound in bulk** — a motion-graphics pass, a whoosh on every text entrance, a tick on every list item, an "add a transition sound to every cut" pass, a template applied across a sequence.
- **When a video tests as "tiring" or "a lot"** with no other explanation, and when average view duration falls off inside the first 2–3 minutes despite good content.
- When retention data shows a dip that does not correspond to a content boundary. Overlay the density trace on the retention curve; a density spike leading a dip by 10–30 seconds is the signature.
- **On a reference video, to record its actual measured density** rather than a description of it. "Busy sound design" is not an observation; `14 SFX/min, median IOI 3.1 s, 62% of accent energy in 2–8 kHz` is. The number is a fingerprint; reproduce it rather than guessing.
- **Not as an argument for silence.** The complementary failure — motion with no sound at all — is [[sfx-unsounded-motion-audit]], and *"when that sound doesn't come, the video feels really hollow, really fake."* This note sets the ceiling; that note sets the floor. The format's budget between them is [[struct-stimulation-budget]].

## How to recognise it in a reference video
**Step 0 — isolate the accent layer.** Everything else is guesswork if the count includes dialogue plosives and music transients. Two routes:

- In **your own** composition, solo the SFX bus: put `data-hidden` on the `voiceover`, `music` and `ambience` `<hf-audio-group>` elements, which *"drops every member from the mix"*, and render. That gives a clean SFX-only file.
- In a **reference** video you cannot solo anything. Narrow the band to where designed accents live and accept the error: `highpass=f=1500,lowpass=f=9000` removes most speech fundamentals and bass music, leaving ticks, clicks, whip cracks and whoosh tops. Report the count as an estimate and say so.

**Step 1 — count, and declare which count you are using.**

*Route A — raw onset census (every transient counts).* ffmpeg-native, using inverted silence detection: each `silence_end` is an onset.
```bash
ffmpeg -i sfx-only.wav -af "silencedetect=n=-40dB:d=0.08" -f null - 2>&1 | grep -c silence_end
# per-minute, with timecodes, for the density trace:
ffmpeg -i sfx-only.wav -af "silencedetect=n=-40dB:d=0.08" -f null - 2>&1 | grep silence_end
```
Where `aubio` is available, `aubioonset -i sfx-only.wav -O specflux -t 0.3` is more accurate on overlapping effects — but it is listed as unverified in this environment, so the `silencedetect` route is the one to specify.

*Route B — designed-accent census (a layered moment counts once).* Short-window peak detection outside the speech band, with merging:
```bash
ffmpeg -i ref.mp4 -vn -ar 48000 ref.wav
# 50 ms windows, peak per window, above and below the voice
ffmpeg -i ref.wav -af "highpass=f=6000,asetnsamples=n=2400,astats=metadata=1:reset=1,ametadata=print:key=lavfi.astats.Overall.Peak_level:file=hi.txt" -f null -
ffmpeg -i ref.wav -af "lowpass=f=200,asetnsamples=n=2400,astats=metadata=1:reset=1,ametadata=print:key=lavfi.astats.Overall.Peak_level:file=lo.txt" -f null -
```
Count windows where the peak rises more than ~8 dB above the running median; **merge detections within 200 ms into one accent.** Divide by runtime in minutes. On your own composition the ledger grep in the Execution spec gives the same count exactly and for free.

**Step 2 — read the band table that matches your count.**

*Route A bands — raw onsets per minute:*

| Onsets/min | Reads as | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| 0–1 | Companionship / vlog / authenticity format | Correct for that promise; suspicious anywhere else |
| 2–4 | Restrained explainer | Fine |
| 5–12 | Standard produced explainer | Fine |
| 13–24 | Entertainment / high-stimulation | Fine **only** if IOI and spectral tests also pass |
| 25–35 | Heavy | Audit every window; expect failures |
| > 35 | **Overload** | Remediate |

*Route B bands — designed accents per minute:*

| Accents/min | Reads as |
|---|---|
| 0–2 | sparse — intimacy formats, or an unsounded-motion problem |
| **3–8** | **the working band for teaching / explainer content** |
| 9–15 | high-energy entertainment; sustainable only in short form |
| 16–25 | approaching "every other second"; fatigue inside 2–3 min |
| > 25 | the named failure. Roughly one accent per 2.4 s or faster |

**Step 3 — inter-onset interval, the real test.** From the onset list, compute the IOI distribution over rolling 60-second windows.
- **Median IOI ≥ 2.5 s** (Route A) / **median gap ≥ 4 s** (Route B) → healthy.
- **Median IOI 1.2–2.5 s** → this is the source's "every other second" zone. Borderline; it fails if sustained for more than about 60 seconds.
- **Median IOI < 1.2 s** sustained over 60 s → overload regardless of the per-minute count.
- **More than 20 % of gaps under 1 s** → the video is dense regardless of its mean.
- **More than 5 accents in any 5-second window** → a local pile-up. Log the timecode; these are usually one over-designed transition.
- **Require at least one effect-free window of 8 s or more per minute.** An ear with no rest habituates fastest.
- **Regularity is its own fault.** Compute the coefficient of variation of the IOIs. A CV **below 0.35** means the accents are near-metronomic, which is exactly the lab condition that produced reported fatigue in six minutes. Irregular accents at the same density tire less.

**Step 4 — spectral clustering.** Measure the share of accent-layer energy in **2–8 kHz**. Above **60%** and every effect is competing in the same band; that is the literal "tick tick tick" the source describes, and re-banding some of them fixes it without deleting anything.

**Step 5 — dynamics.**
```bash
ffmpeg -i mix.wav -af "ebur128=framelog=verbose:peak=true" -f null -
ffmpeg -i mix.wav -af "astats=metadata=1:reset=1:length=1" -f null - 2>&1 | grep -E "Crest|Peak level|Flat factor|Peak count"
```
- **Programme LRA below 5 LU** on a video with visible dynamic intent means the accents have been limited flat. Fatiguing *and* ineffective. A well-mixed talking head sits around **LRA 7–11**; a heavily accented mix shows **LRA above ~14 LU** with a high crest factor and frequent momentary spikes. This is a coarse indicator in both directions — a dynamic score can raise LRA legitimately.
- **Crest factor of the full mix below ~8 dB** is a heavily squashed master. Report it alongside density — high density plus low crest factor is the worst combination.
- **Flat factor** rising indicates clipping-flat samples; any non-zero value on a music-and-effects mix is a mastering fault worth flagging separately.

**Step 6 — salience.** Measure each accent's peak against the nearest dialogue peak. A dense video whose accents sit 18–24 dB down is far less fatiguing than a moderate one whose accents sit 9 dB down. Log rate *and* level together; neither alone predicts fatigue.

**Step 7 — repetition.** Count how many of the onsets are the **same file**. Flag any file used **more than 3 times**, or twice within **90 s**. Where the same effect fires three or more times in a row identically, the source's warning applies directly: *"it sounds really odd, people pick up on it."* Detect it by cross-correlating the 300 ms windows around each onset; near-identical windows (correlation > 0.95) are the same asset unmodified.

**Step 8 — transcript overlay.** Are the accents on *content* beats (a claim, a reveal, a step) or on *housekeeping* (every bullet appearing)? Housekeeping accents are the ones to delete first, and they are usually the majority in an overloaded video.

**Step 9 — correlate with retention.** If a retention curve is available, plot density per 10 s against it. YouTube reads a dip as *"viewers are abandoning or skipping at that specific part"*; a density spike **10–30 seconds before** a dip is the causal shape to look for, since fatigue accrues before the viewer acts on it.

## Reproduction prompt

```
Run the SFX DENSITY AND FATIGUE AUDIT on {{RENDERED_MIX}} / {{COMPOSITION}}
for {{PROJECT}} and remediate. This pass removes work; do not add anything.

1. DECLARE THE COUNT RULE. Route B (designed accents: composition clips, or
   detections merged within 200 ms) is the default. Route A (raw onsets) is
   for reference videos where no ledger exists. Write the choice at the top of
   the audit - every threshold below is route-specific and the two are not
   comparable.
2. BUILD THE ACCENT LEDGER (Route B, own composition). List every SFX and
   design clip with: id, data-start, duration, data-volume, source file, and
   the picture event it serves. Sort by data-start. Use the grep in the
   Execution spec; do not do this by memory.
3. ISOLATE THE ACCENT LAYER (Route A, or to verify the ledger). Set
   data-hidden on the voiceover, music and ambience <hf-audio-group> elements
   and render an SFX-only file. For a reference video you cannot solo,
   band-limit instead:
     ffmpeg -i ref.mp4 -af "highpass=f=1500,lowpass=f=9000" sfx-est.wav
   and mark every number below as an estimate.
4. COUNT ONSETS and record their timecodes:
     ffmpeg -i sfx-only.wav -af "silencedetect=n=-40dB:d=0.08" -f null - 2>&1 | grep silence_end
5. Over rolling 60-second windows stepped every 10 seconds, compute and
   tabulate: accents/min, median inter-onset interval, IOI coefficient of
   variation, share of gaps under 1s, the longest effect-free window, and the
   maximum number of accents in any 5-second sub-window. Then: uses per source
   file, and the spacing between uses of the same file.
6. MEASURE THE FULL MIX:
     ffmpeg -i {{RENDERED_MIX}} -af "ebur128=framelog=verbose:peak=true" -f null -
     ffmpeg -i {{RENDERED_MIX}} -af "astats=metadata=1:reset=1:length=1" -f null -
   Record programme LRA and mix crest factor. Then measure the share of
   accent-layer energy in 2-8 kHz.
7. FLAG any window that fails its route's rate band, OR median IOI/gap floor,
   OR >3 accents in a 5-second sub-window, OR IOI CV < 0.35, OR >20% of gaps
   under 1s, OR no 8s effect-free window in the minute, OR any file used more
   than 3 times or twice within 90s.
8. REMEDIATE each flagged window IN THIS ORDER, and stop as soon as the window
   passes. Do not skip to (d) - deleting is the correct first move.
   (a) DELETE the accents that have no job - housekeeping accents whose
       picture event is a bullet appearing, a minor caption, a small
       reposition, any move under 15% of frame width, or anything whose only
       justification is "there was a transition here". Expect this alone to
       fix most windows; these are the majority of an overloaded ledger.
   (b) THIN - keep every second accent in the run. Do this with
       data-hidden="true" on the individual <audio> clips rather than
       removing them: it hides them in both preview and render, is
       non-destructive and reversible. (Note this vault cannot delete files,
       so hiding is the correct mechanism, not a workaround.)
   (c) DEMOTE - for accents that must stay but need not be noticed, drop
       data-volume from ~0.251 (-12 dB) to 0.1 (-20 dB) and filter them out of
       the 600-4000 Hz band. Rate stays, fatigue falls. Demote before you
       delete anything you actually want.
   (d) VARY every file used more than twice: derive pitch variants (+/-2
       semitones) and length variants (+/-15%) offline, and place the derived
       assets. Similar-sounding substitutes do NOT reset habituation -
       stimulus generalisation means near-identical sounds habituate together
       - so make the variation audible. This fixes repetition fatigue only; it
       does NOT reduce density.
   (e) CLUSTER - where a run of accents is unavoidable, group them into one
       designed moment (2-4 accents inside 1.5s, then nothing for 8s) rather
       than an even tick every second. One event with texture beats eight
       events, and it raises the IOI CV at the same time.
   (f) RE-BAND - if more than 60% of accent energy sits in 2-8 kHz, add a
       lowpass node (f = 3000-5000) to a third of the accents, or pitch them
       down, so the band is shared.
   (g) DUCK - drop the sfx bus 3-6 dB via data-volume on the
       <hf-audio-group>. Last resort: a quiet tick is still a tick.
9. RE-CHECK THE FLOOR: run the unsounded-motion audit afterwards to confirm
   you have not deleted a transition sound or a diegetic sound that was
   carrying the scene. Transitions and location changes keep their sound.
10. RE-RENDER and re-run steps 4-7. Iterate until no window is flagged. Then
    two-pass loudnorm to I=-14, TP=-1.5; expect LRA 7-11 LU.

ACCEPTANCE TEST: (a) every 60-second window passes every gate for its route;
(b) programme LRA >= 6 LU; (c) no unmodified file fires more than twice in a
row; (d) every measurement data-hidden has been removed before the delivery
render. Then watch 3 continuous minutes from the DENSEST part of the video,
once, at normal volume, without pausing, and write down what the section was
about. If you can recall the sound design more easily than the content, the
audit has not gone far enough - delete another 20% of the accents, starting
with the housekeeping ones. Count the accents you consciously noticed: it
should be under 5 in those 3 minutes. Finally watch the FULL runtime once
without pausing - the windowed audit catches piles, only a full watch catches
the cumulative tiredness the source is describing.
```

## Execution spec

**HyperFrames — the audit is mechanical because the ledger is in the DOM.** Every accent is an `<audio>` clip with a `data-start` in seconds, so the whole Route B audit is a query over the composition file:

```bash
# accent ledger straight out of the composition
grep -o '<audio[^>]*data-audio-group="\(sfx\|design\)"[^>]*>' index.html \
  | sed -E 's/.*id="([^"]*)".*src="([^"]*)".*data-start="([^"]*)".*/\3\t\1\t\2/' \
  | sort -n > accents.tsv
awk -F'\t' 'NR>1{print $1-p} {p=$1}' accents.tsv | sort -n | uniq -c   # gap distribution
cut -f3 accents.tsv | sort | uniq -c | sort -rn                        # uses per source file
```
That is the cheapest high-value script in this library: it finds both the rate problem and the repetition problem in one pass, with no listening.

Three contract features carry the remediation, and they are the reason this note is executable at all in a vault that cannot delete files.

**1. The SFX bus.** `<hf-audio-group>` gives *"one chain, one fader, one automation clock for every member"* — the fader is `data-volume` on the bus, applying to every member, and its `data-automation` clock is **composition time**, not clip-local, which is what makes a section-scoped density envelope authorable at all:
```html
<hf-audio-group id="sfx" data-label="Sound effects" data-volume="0.45"
  data-fx-chain="{&quot;version&quot;:1,&quot;nodes&quot;:[
    {&quot;type&quot;:&quot;lowpass&quot;,&quot;id&quot;:&quot;n1&quot;,&quot;label&quot;:&quot;Share the tick band&quot;,
     &quot;params&quot;:{&quot;frequency&quot;:9000,&quot;q&quot;:0.707,&quot;poles&quot;:&quot;2&quot;}},
    {&quot;type&quot;:&quot;limiter&quot;,&quot;id&quot;:&quot;n2&quot;,&quot;params&quot;:{&quot;limit&quot;:-3,&quot;attack&quot;:5,&quot;release&quot;:50}}]}"></hf-audio-group>
```
Chain order is signal order and the doctrine is *"subtract before you add, level after you filter, relationships after level, character and ceiling last"* — limiter last, as a ceiling. **`data-fx-carve` must never go on a bus** (`audio_group_carve_attr`), and `compressor`/`limiter`/`gate`/`bitcrush` have **zero automatable parameters** (they are AudioWorklets configured wholesale); automate a `gain` stage around one instead. **Demoting a class in one move** is the same mechanism: put every non-essential accent in `data-audio-group="design"` and drop that one fader — an 8 dB salience reduction across the whole layer without touching 60 clips.

**2. Soloing for measurement.** `data-hidden` on a bus *"drops every member from the mix"*. Setting it on the `voiceover`, `music` and `ambience` groups and rendering is how you get a clean accent-only file to count. Remember to remove it before the delivery render — nothing lints for a forgotten `data-hidden`.

**3. Thinning without deleting.** `data-hidden="true"` on an individual clip *"hides the element in both preview and render, overriding its time window"*, is **non-destructive and reversible**, and is toggled by Studio's eye icon. Given the execution contract's first constraint — *"the mounted vault folder cannot delete files"* — hiding is not a shortcut, it is the correct primitive for a reversible thinning pass, it makes an A/B one attribute away, and it leaves the decision auditable:
```html
<audio id="sfx-whoosh-07" src="assets/sfx/whoosh-air-02.wav" data-audio-group="sfx"
       data-start="132.4" data-duration="0.6" data-track-index="12"
       data-hidden="true"></audio>   <!-- thinned: 5 accents in 4s window at 131-135 -->
```
Always leave the comment. A hidden clip with no reason recorded gets un-hidden by the next pass.

Other constraints in force: every `<audio>` needs an `id` (id-less → **never mixed** → silent render); overlapping audio on one `data-track-index` raises `duplicate_audio_track`, so give each member of a cluster its own index; a `volume` automation lane beats a GSAP `volume` tween silently (`audio_volume_double_automation`); **variation cannot be done in-composition** — `data-playback-rate` is a constant `0.1..5` and is **pitch-preserved** for sound, there is no pitch node in the FX registry and no rate envelope, so real pitch variants must be preprocessed and placed as separate assets. **Nothing checks any of this.** *"Almost no static gate covers the mix"* — the linter reads `data-automation` for exactly two conflicts and validates neither chains nor lanes. This audit *is* the gate; script it into the pre-render checklist.

**ffmpeg — the census and the variation factory.** All measurement, no re-encode of picture.
```bash
# Route A onsets (each silence_end is an onset)
ffmpeg -i sfx-only.wav -af "silencedetect=n=-40dB:d=0.08" -f null - 2>&1 | grep silence_end
# Route B accent census (see recognition section for the window arithmetic)
ffmpeg -i ref.wav -af "highpass=f=6000,asetnsamples=n=2400,astats=metadata=1:reset=1,ametadata=print:key=lavfi.astats.Overall.Peak_level:file=hi.txt" -f null -
# loudness range, true peak, momentary/short-term series
ffmpeg -i mix.wav -af "ebur128=framelog=verbose:peak=true" -f null - 2>&1 | grep -E ' M:| LRA:'
# crest factor, peak count, flat factor in 1-second windows
ffmpeg -i mix.wav -af "astats=metadata=1:reset=1:length=1" -f null - 2>&1 | grep -E "Crest|Peak count|Flat factor"
# accent-band estimate on a reference you cannot solo
ffmpeg -i ref.mp4 -af "highpass=f=1500,lowpass=f=9000" -ar 48000 sfx-est.wav
# audible variation: +/-2 semitones, length preserved
ffmpeg -i whoosh.wav -af "rubberband=pitch=1.122" whoosh.up2.wav
ffmpeg -i whoosh.wav -af "rubberband=pitch=0.891" whoosh.dn2.wav
# length variation +/-15% (tape-style, pitch moves with it - often better on effects)
ffmpeg -i whoosh.wav -af "asetrate=48000*1.15,aresample=48000" whoosh.short.wav
ffmpeg -i whoosh.wav -af "atempo=0.87" whoosh.long.wav
```
Register each derived file so it is reusable: `node <SKILL_DIR>/scripts/resolve.mjs --from whoosh.up2.wav --type sfx --project .`

For delivery, normalise with two-pass `loudnorm` and `linear=true` so a constant gain is applied and the mix's own dynamics are preserved — a dynamic pass will squash the accents further and lower LRA below the floor this note sets.

**Epidemic Sound — the anti-repetition fetch.** The audit's output is often a shopping list: "this whoosh fires six times identically, get three more." The catalogue answer to mistake number three is to pull a *set*, not a file:
```
SearchSimilarToSoundEffect { id: <the whoosh you already used> }   # 4-6 siblings in one call
SearchSoundEffects {
  filter: { tagSlugs: { matchType: ANY, values: ["swooshes--whoosh","swooshes--swish"] },
            duration: { min: 300, max: 1200 } },
  sort: { by: POPULARITY, order: DESCENDING }, first: 20 }
```
`filter.duration {min,max}` is in **milliseconds**, and `filter.tagSlugs {matchType: "NOT_ANY", values: ["loop"]}` keeps sustained material out of a short-accent search. Fetch 4–6 variants of any effect you expect to use more than twice, at fetch time, before building. Retrofitting variety after the fact is the expensive path — and note the source's own framing of why the library matters: on free sources *"you don't get many variants of one sound effect, you don't get a lot of sound effects with a similar texture."* Variants are the product feature that fixes this mistake. Sourcing produces a file and stops; placement is HyperFrames.

**Remotion:** the same ledger, derived from the `<Audio>` elements' `from` frames; the same measurement applies to any rendered mix. Concept only; no Remotion runtime in this project.

## Failure modes
- **Comparing a Route A number against a Route B threshold.** "22 per minute" is a healthy entertainment mix by the onset table and a named failure by the accent table. Fix: declare the count rule at the top of the audit and carry it through every number.
- **Auditing one sample instead of the runtime.** Fatigue is cumulative; a clean 60 seconds proves nothing about minute seven. Fix: rolling windows across the whole file, tabulated.
- **Counting onsets in the full mix.** Dialogue plosives and music transients inflate the count by a factor of three or more, and the resulting "overload" finding sends you deleting effects that were never the problem. Fix: solo the bus with `data-hidden`, or band-limit and label the number an estimate.
- **Sounding every motion because every motion "expects" a sound.** The expectation is real; satisfying all of it is the failure. Fix: hold the rate band, and delete housekeeping accents first.
- **Ducking first.** Turning the SFX bus down is the reflex and the least effective fix: a quiet tick every second is still a tick every second, and now it is also indistinct. Fix: follow the remediation order — delete, thin, demote, vary, cluster, re-band, and only then duck.
- **Fixing repetition and calling it density.** Swapping in three distinct whooshes removes the "people pick up on it" problem and leaves the density identical. Fix: they are separate gates; both must pass.
- **Fixing repetition by substituting a near-identical file.** Stimulus generalisation means similar sounds habituate together, so nothing is regained. Fix: make the variation audible — ±2 semitones and ±15 % length, or a different family entirely.
- **Judging density by the mean only.** A video at 6/min with all six inside ten seconds is dense. Fix: check the gap distribution and require a rest window.
- **Risers everywhere.** They habituate exactly like any other repeated cue and then *"lose their credibility and stop working"* — after which the one that mattered does nothing. Fix: reserve them; count them.
- **Reducing rate but keeping salience.** Twelve accents at −9 dB is more fatiguing than twenty at −20 dB. Fix: demote before you delete anything you actually want.
- **Deleting the diegetic effects.** In a thinning pass the easiest cuts are the ones tied to something visible — a click, a door, a keystroke — and those are the effects the video cannot do without. Fix: thin the *aesthetic* layer first; diegetic and motion effects are the last to go.
- **Cutting so hard the motion layer disappears.** The opposite named failure: *"the video feels really hollow, really fake."* Fix: run [[sfx-unsounded-motion-audit]] after this pass; transitions and location changes always keep their sound.
- **Limiting the accents flat to control the density feel.** Drops programme LRA under 5 LU, which is itself a documented fatigue cause. Fix: fewer accents, not squashed ones.
- **Auditing by memory.** Nobody can hold 200 accent timings in their head, and *"working memory is extremely limited in both capacity and duration"* applies to editors too. Fix: generate the ledger from the composition file with the grep above.
- **Leaving a measurement `data-hidden` in place.** Soloing the SFX bus by hiding the voice group and then forgetting to un-hide it delivers a video with no dialogue, and **nothing lints for it**. Fix: make un-hiding an explicit acceptance-test line before the delivery render.
- **Known gap:** no published study fixes an accents-per-minute fatigue threshold, so the density bands are calibrated from the source's own "every other second → tired in 2–3 minutes" claim plus the habituation and cognitive-load mechanisms. The defensible parts are the mechanisms, the measurement procedure, and the fact that rate, IOI, repetition, salience and spectrum are independent axes. Treat the bands as profile-relative: measure the reference, then match it.
- **Known gap:** nothing in this stack measures the mix at author time. `ebur128`, `astats` and `silencedetect` all run on rendered audio; the render is browser-hosted (an `OfflineAudioContext` in headless Chrome) and browser-dependent, so on this linux ARM64 VM without sudo it must happen elsewhere. The audit is therefore a **post-render loop**, and its numbers must be written back into the design document so the next pass starts from them rather than re-deriving them.
